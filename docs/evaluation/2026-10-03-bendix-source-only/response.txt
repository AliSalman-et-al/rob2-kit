### D2.6 — Was an appropriate analysis used to estimate the effect of assignment?

**Answer: No.** The stated target is the effect of assignment among all randomized participants. Instead, the reported comparison includes only the 45 treated and 49 control participants who returned for follow-up; the authors state that the two who never started treatment and seven who dropped out were not included, and only two of those nine could be contacted at four months (p. 6, lines 83–94). The result likewise reports 29/45 versus 14/49 able to work (p. 5, lines 85–90). This is not an analysis of everyone as randomized. Under the supplied guidance, excluding eligible participants after randomization is inappropriate unless the exclusion is solely for missing outcome data in an otherwise appropriate modified intention-to-treat analysis.

**Uncertainty and counterevidence:** The article does not establish for every excluded participant whether the four-month outcome was unavailable or whether any were ineligible; only two of the nine were contactable at follow-up (p. 6, lines 89–94). That limits certainty about the exact exclusion mechanism. However, the flow and reported analysis population show that randomized treated participants were omitted; the supplied evidence does not establish an eligible-participant exclusion exception or an analysis retaining all randomized participants by assignment. The matching of control follow-up intervals to treated participants (p. 5, lines 73–79) does not resolve those exclusions.

### D2.7 — Conditional on the D2.6 answer

**Answer: Yes, there was potential for substantial impact.** Nine of 55 randomized to treatment were excluded from the reported comparison (about 16% of that arm), versus two of 51 controls not represented in the follow-up counts shown in the flow diagram (p. 3, lines 4–13, 19–31; p. 6, lines 83–94). The excluded treated participants included two who never started and seven who dropped out; the reported reasons include increased back pain, finding the program too difficult, and other circumstances (p. 3, lines 8–13; p. 4, lines 4–12). These factors could plausibly relate to the ability-to-work outcome. The outcome was not so rare among analysed participants—64% versus 28%—but the exclusion count is substantial, particularly in the treatment arm (p. 5, lines 83–90).

**Uncertainty and counterevidence:** Outcomes for most of the nine excluded treated participants are not reported, and the source does not establish their eligibility or ability to work; their actual effect on the estimate therefore cannot be determined (p. 6, lines 89–94). The authors report that dropouts did not differ from graduates on measured characteristics (p. 4, lines 9–12), which weighs against assuming a large prognostic difference, but does not establish comparable work outcomes or remove the potential impact of excluding them.
