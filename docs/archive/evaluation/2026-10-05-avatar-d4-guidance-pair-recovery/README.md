## Scientific limits and unresolved reasoning

There is observable component-weighted reasoning and better citation locality in the
corrected run, but **no demonstrated accuracy gain or causal intervention effect**.
Delivered 4.2 wording was identical across arms (the intervention changed 4.1/4.4
composite qualification and 4.5 contextual reasoning). Different 4.2 answers activated
different branches; old 4.5 was not assessed. Stochastic interpretation and the combined
contextual/composite intervention cannot be separated by this single pair. The results
do not establish that High was wrong or Some concerns was right.

Independent source review corrected an initial audit overstatement: Table 4 upper block
is labeled Primary end point (all-cause death + MACE), not literally first events. Its
totals 26/13 match the primary analysis, but all25 deaths also appear in the lower
repeated-MACE block. Supplementary Table 4(PDF 15–17) gives dates of death and causes
without chronology relative to prior composite events or a component accounting rule.
These are reported primary-endpoint component counts; 25 chronologically first deaths
are not verified. The model output makes that stronger first-event inference, which
remains frozen and is a claim-fidelity limitation.

Both 4.2 positions need scrutiny. Earlier surgery is a real exposure capable of causing
events as well as a possible detection opportunity. Genuine prevention of HF or
perioperative causation of events is a treatment effect, not measurement bias. The old
inference is plausible but component-specific monitoring was not established. Supplied
postoperative Table S3(PDF 14) records AMI 0/72 versus 0/25 and stroke/TIA 2 versus 0.
Zero postoperative AMI counts weaken a material perioperative MI explanation; combined
stroke/TIA is not the stroke-only primary component. Neither observation proves
comparable ascertainment. The old surveillance claim was expressly an inference, not a
fabricated source quotation. The corrected No may overstate reassurance from equal
scheduled follow-up: perioperative ascertainment remains unreported, and the supplied
periprocedural biomarker criterion matters. Neither routine visits nor an acute-
clinical-event label resolves that pathway alone.

Corrected 4.5 distinguishes possibility/likelihood and weights reported component
frequency, but still cites absence of reported actual influence as reassurance. Protocol
criteria and consensus review provide contextual counterevidence; the main article(PDF
2) explicitly calls the DSMB independent, and PDF 4 explicitly states it was unblinded.
Institutional independence is distinct from blinding; neither standard definitions nor
consensus establishes absence of allocation-influenced thresholds. The source describes
clinical HF admission decisions as well as DSMB classification; relevant care-provider
interests, expectations and comparator context are not fully appraised. Dominant
objective events do not erase those concerns. Table 4also records8 HF and6 MI/stroke
primary-endpoint component events. HFcounts differ7 versus 1, while deaths differ16
versus 9: frequency alone does not characterize contribution to the between-arm
contrast. These crude counts cannot decompose the primary hazard ratio, but show why a
complete influential-component argument should not stop at 25/39 objective events. No
hand-labeled replacement is imposed and neither vector is changed after observing
results.
